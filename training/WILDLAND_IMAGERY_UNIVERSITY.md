# Wildland Imagery University

## Teaching terrain judgment before the emergency

**Status: TRAINING CONCEPT / PROJECT-DEVELOPED MATERIAL**

This branch exists for a reason that is bigger than software:

> **Giving a firefighter excellent imagery is only half the job. The other half is teaching the firefighter how to read it.**

Modern aerial and satellite imagery can reveal access, terrain, vegetation patterns, roads, drainage, clearcuts, structures, obstacles, and changes over time. But the image does not automatically supply judgment.

Experienced firefighters learn to notice things that a new firefighter may look directly at and never recognize.

Wildland Imagery University is a concept for capturing that reasoning and teaching it systematically.

---

# SEE → THINK → DECIDE

The training model is deliberately simple.

## SEE

Recognize what is physically visible.

Examples:

- road versus trail;
- road width;
- turnaround potential;
- gates;
- bridges and culverts;
- swamp / standing water;
- clearcuts;
- logging roads;
- plantations;
- burn scars;
- power lines;
- dozer lines;
- structures;
- fence lines;
- vehicle tracks;
- smoke columns;
- drainage and terrain form.

## THINK

Ask why an experienced ranger cares about that feature.

Is the road really drivable?

Can a dozer turn around?

Could a mapped road actually be an abandoned trail?

Does a bridge create a weight or width problem?

Does vegetation hide standing water?

Is the apparent access route also the only escape route?

What does season or recent disturbance change?

## DECIDE

Use the observation to improve a field decision before committing resources.

Examples:

- approach route;
- staging location;
- dozer access;
- escape route;
- resource type;
- whether another reconnaissance source is needed;
- whether the map and ground reality agree closely enough to proceed.

---

## Core principle

> **Maps tell you where things should be. Experience tells you what they are really like.**

Technology should reinforce fieldcraft, not replace it.

Firefighters still need to drive their response areas, learn local roads, gates, bridges, water sources, terrain, seasonal changes, and the difference between a mapped feature and its real-world condition.

Imagery becomes more valuable when experienced people teach others what they are seeing.

---

## Questions every training image should ask

A good imagery exercise should not begin by labeling everything for the student.

Start with questions:

1. **Would you drive through this?**
2. **What concerns you first?**
3. **Where is your escape route?**
4. **What would a new firefighter likely miss?**
5. **What additional information do you need before committing equipment?**
6. **What feature looks different when you zoom out?**
7. **What feature looks different when you zoom in?**
8. **What appears mapped but may not be usable on the ground?**

The point is not to memorize icons. The point is to build a repeatable way of looking.

## Change elevation to change the question

One of the project-developed training ideas is to treat viewing elevation as an operational thinking tool.

**These ranges are project training heuristics, not agency doctrine or a substitute for local procedures.**

### Strategic view — roughly 10,000–20,000 ft

Look for:

- major roads;
- rivers and lakes;
- towns / developed areas;
- large terrain form;
- fire perimeter / large smoke column when visible;
- regional relationship between incident and resources.

Questions:

- Where is this incident in the big picture?
- What are the major approach corridors?
- Where can resources stage broadly?
- What terrain or water features constrain movement?

### Operational view — roughly 2,000–5,000 ft

Look for:

- logging-road networks;
- clearcuts;
- swamps;
- plantations;
- burn scars;
- utility corridors;
- large dozer lines;
- access/escape relationships.

Questions:

- Which routes actually reach the incident area?
- Where could resources transition from road travel to off-road work?
- What looks like an access trap?
- Where would you stage before committing closer?

### Tactical view — roughly 300–1,000 ft

Look for:

- road width;
- turnarounds;
- berms;
- washouts;
- small bridges;
- sand / wet areas;
- local obstructions;
- vehicle-scale access.

Questions:

- Can this specific vehicle get there?
- Can it turn around?
- Can it get back out?
- Is there room for more than one piece of equipment?

### Detail view — roughly 50–200 ft

Look for:

- gates;
- fence lines;
- individual structures;
- culverts;
- fallen trees when imagery allows;
- vehicle tracks;
- small obstacles;
- exact feature location.

Questions:

- What am I actually looking at?
- Does the high-level interpretation survive close inspection?
- What small obstacle could defeat the whole route plan?

## Zoom like you breathe

The project phrase is:

> **Zoom out. Form a hypothesis. Zoom in. Confirm. Zoom back out. Repeat.**

One elevation rarely tells the whole story.

A road can be obvious when close and meaningless without regional context.

A clearcut can look like open access when close but reveal drainage or isolation when viewed wider.

A mapped trail can look important on a street layer and disappear into vegetation on imagery.

The skill is moving continuously between context and detail.

---

## Why hybrid imagery matters

A recurring project observation is that straight imagery and straight street/cartographic maps each answer different questions.

### Imagery

Shows ground truth better:

- vegetation;
- actual clearings;
- visible roads and tracks;
- terrain texture;
- structures;
- recent disturbance when imagery is current.

But road names and network relationships may be difficult to identify quickly.

### Street / labeled map

Shows named network structure clearly.

But a mapped line may represent a trail, obsolete route, or feature whose real-world condition is not obvious.

### Hybrid

Combines the two views so the firefighter can cross-check:

```text
mapped road network
+
visible ground condition
→ better question
```

Hybrid imagery does not guarantee a road is passable. It makes discrepancies easier to notice.

---

## More than zoom: patterns teach experience

### Season matters

The same area may tell a different story after heavy rain, drought, logging, fire, leaf-off conditions, or seasonal vegetation change.

### Shadows are information

Shadows can reveal height, ditch depth, ridge shape, tree structure, and building form—but can also hide detail.

### Human geometry is a clue

Straight lines, repeated spacing, right angles, parallel tracks, rectangular clearings, and regular patterns often indicate human-built features.

Nature can be geometric too, so this is a clue, not a verdict.

### Look for relationships, not isolated objects

A bridge matters because of what road feeds it.

A gate matters because of what lies beyond it.

A swamp matters because it changes route choices.

A turnaround matters because a long narrow approach without one can become a commitment problem.

---

## The instructor is experience

The best source material for this training is not generic AI-generated advice.

It is experienced firefighters sitting over real imagery and explaining:

- what catches their eye first;
- what makes them uncomfortable;
- what they would verify before sending a crew;
- what a younger firefighter commonly misses;
- how their interpretation changes with local knowledge;
- what happened when a map feature did not match the ground.

AI can help organize those observations, find recurring categories, create exercises, build diagrams, and preserve the reasoning.

It should not invent field experience it does not possess.

## A proposed lesson format

Each lesson can be small.

```text
REAL IMAGE
→ no labels at first
→ 3–5 questions
→ student interpretation
→ experienced-firefighter reasoning
→ annotated answer image
→ what changed the decision?
```

Possible lesson themes:

- **Would You Drive This?**
- **Find the Turnaround**
- **Road or Trail?**
- **Where Is the Water?**
- **Spot the Hidden Access Problem**
- **Choose an Escape Route**
- **What Changed Since the Map Was Made?**
- **Read the Clearcut**
- **Read the Swamp**
- **Read the Shadows**
- **Find the Human Pattern**
- **Strategic vs Tactical View**

A library of real local examples can become more valuable than a generic software tutorial.

## Relationship to the technology projects

Offline GeoStack, Rasta Pyramid Factory, Map Fountain, ArcGIS Earth, and Field Maps solve parts of the **information delivery** problem.

Wildland Imagery University addresses the **understanding** problem.

```text
Factory / imagery sources
→ useful local imagery
→ field device
→ trained observer
→ better-informed decision
```

The trained observer is the important component.

---

## Safety and authority boundary

This project is an independent training concept, not official agency doctrine.

It does not replace:

- agency policy;
- incident command;
- local operating procedures;
- current fire behavior information;
- scouting / reconnaissance;
- road-condition verification;
- established wildland safety principles;
- the judgment of qualified personnel on scene.

Imagery can be stale, incomplete, seasonally misleading, obscured, or simply wrong for the current condition.

The purpose is to improve observation and questioning—not to make remote imagery the final authority.

# The long-term idea

Sit experienced firefighters down with real imagery.

Ask them what they see.

Ask why it matters.

Capture the reasoning.

Teach it forward.

> **One image can start a conversation. A thousand conversations can build a university.**
